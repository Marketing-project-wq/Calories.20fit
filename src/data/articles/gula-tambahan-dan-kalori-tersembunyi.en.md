Many people are already fairly careful about rice and fried food, yet still struggle to lose weight because of one calorie source that often escapes notice: added sugar, especially in liquid form — sweet drinks, trendy coffee, and packaged beverages. This article covers the difference between natural and added sugar, why liquid calories feel "like they don't count" psychologically, and how to spot hidden sugar on packaging labels.

## Natural Sugar vs. Added Sugar: What's the Difference?

Not all sugar is equal in a health context, even though they're chemically similar:

- **Natural (intrinsic) sugar** is sugar that occurs naturally inside whole foods, like fructose in fruit or lactose in milk. This type comes packaged with fiber, water, vitamins, and minerals that slow its absorption and add to the food's overall nutritional value.
- **Added sugar** is sugar or sweetener added during the production or preparation of food/drinks — including table sugar, palm sugar, added honey, high-fructose corn syrup, and similar. This sugar generally comes without accompanying fiber or nutrients, so it's absorbed faster and contributes "empty" calories (energy with little nutritional value).

The WHO recommends limiting added sugar to **less than 10% of total daily energy**, with additional health benefits if it can be reduced below 5%. For an average intake of 2,000 kcal/day, that's roughly 50 grams (about 12 teaspoons) as the upper limit, or ideally under 25 grams.

## Why Liquid Calories Feel "Like They Don't Count"

One consistent finding in nutrition research is that calories from drinks tend to trigger less fullness than the same amount of calories from solid food. A few reasons behind this:

- **Faster transit time** — liquid passes through the stomach much faster than solid food, so the fullness signal to the brain is weaker and shorter-lived.
- **Lack of chewing** — chewing solid food gives the body time to register incoming energy; drinks skip this process entirely.
- **Lower thermic effect** — the body burns less energy processing liquid sugar than digesting fiber-rich solid food.

As a result, someone who drinks a glass of a sweet beverage (which can add 150–250 kcal) tends not to reduce their next meal to compensate — those calories become a "pure addition" on top of daily energy needs, not a substitute for calories from another source.

> A glass of packaged sweet iced tea or palm-sugar milk coffee can add 150–300 kcal — equivalent to more than a scoop of white rice, but with far less lasting fullness.

> Long-term excess added sugar consumption is linked to risk of type 2 diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, tooth decay, and heart disease — effects that only become visible after years, not a reason to avoid sugar entirely, but a strong reason not to underestimate a daily sweet drink habit.

Low/zero-calorie alternative sweeteners (stevia, sucralose, aspartame, erythritol) are considered safe within reasonable limits by the WHO and other health authorities, and can help reduce total added sugar intake — but they're not a magic weight-loss solution, since some people tend to "compensate" by eating more elsewhere, and constantly maintaining a strong sweet taste preference can make natural food feel less satisfying. Plain water remains the best choice over relying on any sweetener.

## Hidden Sugar Sources That Often Go Unnoticed

Added sugar isn't only found in obviously sweet food. Some commonly overlooked sources: packaged sauces and condiments (sweet soy sauce, bottled chili sauce, salad dressing), packaged bread that doesn't taste sweet, "healthy" drinks labeled less sugar or flavored yogurt drinks, granola and breakfast cereal marketed as healthy choices, and trendy coffee with syrup, sweet creamer, and boba.

> As a reference point, one bottle of packaged sweet iced tea (~450 ml) can add 8-10 teaspoons of sugar, and one cup of trendy palm-sugar milk coffee can reach 10-14 teaspoons — approaching or exceeding the WHO's daily limit (~12 tsp) from a single drink, before counting sugar from anything else eaten that day.

## How to Read a Label to Spot Added Sugar

On Indonesian food packaging, sugar information appears on the Nutrition Facts label (ING), but it takes a bit of care to read properly:

1. **Check the ingredient list, not just the sugar number on the nutrition table.** Added sugar often shows up under other names: sucrose, glucose, high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), maltose, dextrose, honey, palm sugar, coconut sugar — all of these still count as added sugar even though they sound "more natural".
2. **Pay attention to ingredient order.** Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight, so if sugar (in any form) appears in the top two or three, the product tends to be high in sugar.
3. **Compare "sugar per serving" against the actual serving size.** Some packaging lists a small serving size (like half a bottle), making the printed sugar number look low even though a full package contains double that.
4. **Watch out for "no added sugar" or "less sugar" claims.** These claims are regulation-valid, but don't mean low calorie — the product can still contain a large amount of natural sugar from fruit or other sweeteners that still contribute calories.

Importantly: the recommendation to limit added sugar **does not apply** to natural sugar in whole fruit, vegetables, or plain milk — the WHO explicitly excludes these, since the fiber, water, and micronutrients that come with them slow sugar absorption and provide fullness. Fruit juice still needs to be limited more strictly than whole fruit, since the juicing process removes most of the fiber and concentrates sugar from multiple pieces of fruit at once.

## Sugar Doesn't Have to Be Avoided Entirely

Limiting added sugar isn't the same as avoiding it completely or treating it as "poison". Sugar remains a safe energy source in reasonable amounts as part of a balanced diet — health problems arise from long-term excessive consumption patterns, not from one occasional sweet treat. A more realistic, sustainable approach:

- Prioritize plain water as your main drink, and treat sweet drinks as occasional, not daily.
- Choose whole fruit over juice when you want natural sweetness, and get in the habit of reading packaging labels before buying.
- Gradually lower how sweet your drinks are — for example, going from "regular sugar" to "half sugar" for a few weeks first — which is much easier to sustain than jumping straight to zero sugar, since your taste buds need time to adapt.

## Summary

Added sugar, especially in drink form, is one of the most common hidden calorie sources because it's low in fullness and easy to overconsume without noticing, with long-term effects touching type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease, and dental health. The WHO recommends staying under 10% of total daily calories, ideally under 5% — roughly equal to 6 teaspoons for an average calorie need. Recognizing sugar's other names on ingredient labels, paying attention to actual serving size, and prioritizing plain water over sweet drinks are the most practical steps to controlling this hidden calorie source without having to avoid natural sugar from fruit and vegetables entirely.
