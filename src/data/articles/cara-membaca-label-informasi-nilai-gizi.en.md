The Nutrition Facts label (ING) on food packaging is actually one of the most practical tools for controlling calorie intake, but most people just glance at it without really understanding how to read it. This article covers the ING's components in full according to BPOM regulations, how to avoid the misleading serving-size trap, and what claims like "low fat" or "no added sugar" officially mean.

This skill is becoming more relevant as more packaged food and ready-to-drink options fill the Indonesian market, each with marketing claims that are sometimes more convincing than the actual nutrition content — from school snacks to "healthy" menu items at fast food chains.

## Legal Basis and Required Components of Nutrition Labels in Indonesia

In Indonesia, nutrition labeling on processed food is regulated by BPOM, referencing the Angka Kecukupan Gizi (AKG) from Ministry of Health Regulation No. 28 of 2019 as the basis for calculating daily-need percentages. The components generally required on a Nutrition Facts label include:

1. **Serving size** — the amount of food considered one reasonable portion.
2. **Servings per package** — how many portions are contained in one full package.
3. **Total energy** (kcal) per serving, sometimes also listing energy from fat.
4. **Total fat, saturated fat, and trans fat** (if present).
5. **Protein.**
6. **Total carbohydrates, including sugar and dietary fiber.**
7. **Sodium.**
8. **%AKG (%DV)** — the percentage contribution of one serving to daily needs, based on a 2,150 kcal reference.

## Trap Number One: Unrealistic Serving Sizes

This is the most common mistake when reading a nutrition label. Manufacturers are allowed to set serving sizes according to their product category's standard, and this number **doesn't always match how much people actually eat in one sitting** — a bag of chips that looks like "one snack portion" might actually say "3 servings per package," meaning if you finish it in one go, you're consuming 3× the calories listed on the ING. The same goes for a 500 ml bottle labeled "serving size 250 ml (2 servings per package)" — drink the whole thing and the sugar and calories are double what you glanced at.

**How to avoid it**: always check "servings per package" first before looking at the calorie number, then multiply based on the portion you'll actually consume.

## Understanding %AKG (%DV) Correctly

The %AKG (Percentage of Daily Nutritional Adequacy) shows how much one serving contributes to daily needs, calculated based on a general 2,150 kcal/day reference (with different references for other nutrients). This reference is a general average, not a personal requirement — people with higher or lower calorie needs should adjust their interpretation accordingly. Quick guide: 5% DV or less per serving is considered low, 20% DV or more is considered high, applying to nutrients like saturated fat, sugar, and sodium.

## Carbohydrates: Check the Sugar and Fiber Breakdown, Not Just the Total

The "total carbohydrate" number alone isn't very informative — two products can have the same total carbs but very different nutritional quality depending on the breakdown: the higher the sugar portion, the lower the overall nutritional value, while the higher the fiber content, the better. Whole wheat bread and white bread can have similar total carbs per slice, but whole wheat bread usually has far more fiber and less added sugar — a difference only visible if you check the breakdown, not just the total.

For fat, distinguish between types: saturated fat should ideally be kept under 10% of total daily energy (WHO), since excess intake is linked to cardiovascular risk, while trans fat should ideally be avoided entirely — though regulations increasingly restrict it, some fried products and certain margarines can still contain it.

Sodium is also often overlooked, even though excess intake is linked to long-term hypertension risk. The WHO recommends staying under **2,000 mg of sodium per day** (about ~5 grams of table salt). Processed food, instant noodles, and salty snacks are often the biggest unnoticed contributors — even food that doesn't taste particularly salty on the tongue can still be high in sodium due to preservatives and flavor enhancers.

## What Front-of-Package Claims Really Mean — Don't Always Take Them at Face Value

Claims like "low fat," "high fiber," or "no added sugar" printed prominently on the front are officially regulated and must meet certain thresholds, but a few things to keep in mind: "no added sugar" doesn't mean low calorie (the product can still have a lot of natural sugar from fruit concentrate), and "low fat" is sometimes compensated with more added sugar to maintain taste. Front-of-package claims are regulation-valid, but still worth verifying against the ING table on the back.

> Example: two cereal boxes — Product A shows "100 kcal/serving" with a big "low fat" claim on front, Product B shows "150 kcal/serving" with no claim. Calculated per gram, the calorie density actually turns out similar — and if Product A has 8 grams of sugar per serving (compensating for low fat with more sugar, a common industry pattern) while Product B has only 3 grams, Product B — the one that looks "higher calorie" on paper — could actually be the better nutritional choice. Reading just one number without the full ING breakdown can be misleading.

Special claims like "gluten-free," "low sugar," "high fiber," or "organic" each have a specific technical meaning under regulation and aren't a general guarantee that "this product is healthy" — for example, "gluten-free" isn't automatically healthier for people without celiac disease (the product sometimes adds sugar/fat to replace the texture gluten provided), and "organic" concerns the production method, not nutritional content.

Beyond the ING table, the ingredient list also matters: ingredients are listed from most to least by weight, many food additives are listed under technical names (sodium benzoate, MSG) that have already been safety-evaluated by BPOM, and "natural"-sounding ingredients like honey or palm sugar are still classified as added sugar nutritionally.

## Quick Practical Steps for Reading a Label

1. Check the serving size and servings per package first — adjust to the portion you'll actually eat, not the raw number printed.
2. Look at the energy (kcal) per serving adjusted to your actual portion, not just the big number on the front.
3. Check the sugar and fiber breakdown under carbohydrates, not just the total — two products with the same total carbs can be very different in quality.
4. Pay attention to sodium, especially for processed and ready-to-eat food, which are often the biggest hidden contributors.
5. Glance at the ingredient list — check the order and recognize other names for added sugar.
6. Use %AKG as a quick comparison between similar products, but adjust for your personal calorie needs rather than taking the 2,150 kcal reference at face value.

## Summary

The Nutrition Facts label carries more information than it appears at first glance, and the most common trap isn't a wrong calorie number — it's misreading the serving size and servings per package. By understanding the sugar, fiber, fat, and sodium breakdown, checking the ingredient list, and using %AKG to compare similar products — rather than just the total numbers and big front-of-package claims — choosing packaged food can be far more accurate. This skill takes a bit of practice at first, but once it becomes habit, reading a full label only takes a few extra seconds compared to just glancing at the calorie count.
