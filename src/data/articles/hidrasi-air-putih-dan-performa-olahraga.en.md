Plain water is rarely thought of as part of a "nutrition strategy", yet hydration status affects nearly everything related to physical performance, concentration, and even how hungry you feel. This article covers how much fluid you actually need daily, how mild dehydration affects exercise performance, and debunks myths around "drinking water makes you fat" or "everyone needs 8 glasses a day".

## Why Hydration Matters Beyond Just "Feeling Thirsty"

Water makes up about 50–60% of an adult's body weight and is involved in nearly every physiological process: transporting nutrients and oxygen to cells, removing metabolic waste through the kidneys, lubricating joints, and regulating body temperature through sweat. Thirst itself is a relatively late signal — by the time you start feeling thirsty, your body is already in a state of mild fluid deficit.

## How Much Fluid Do You Actually Need Daily?

The popular "8 glasses a day" (about 2 liters) figure is actually a rough generalization, not a recommendation based on individual needs. Actual fluid needs are shaped by many factors: body weight, environmental temperature and humidity, physical activity level, and how much fluid you already get from food.

A more realistic reference:

- The **National Academies of Sciences (US)** recommends around 3.7 liters of total fluid per day for adult men and 2.7 liters for adult women — this includes fluid from all sources, not just plain water, since about 20% of the average person's daily fluid needs are already met through food (especially fruit and vegetables).
- **Indonesia's hot, humid climate** increases fluid loss through sweat compared to temperate countries, so fluid needs tend to sit at the higher end of that range, especially for people who are active outdoors.
- **The most practical indicator**: urine color. Pale yellow to clear urine generally indicates adequate hydration; dark yellow means you need more fluids.

> Fluid needs aren't a single number that's the same for everyone — urine color and thirst are more personalized, practical indicators than a rigid "8 glasses" benchmark.

## Mild Dehydration and Its Effect on Performance

Sports physiology research consistently finds that even mild dehydration — around **2% of body weight** lost through fluid (roughly 1.4 kg for someone weighing 70 kg) — is enough to measurably reduce performance: endurance drops because blood volume decreases, muscle strength drops during prolonged activity, thermoregulation is disrupted so the risk of heat exhaustion rises, and cognitive functions like concentration and alertness also decline.

Practical strategy around exercise time: drink adequately a few hours before a session (enough for light-colored urine), plain water for sessions under an hour, electrolyte drinks for sessions longer than an hour especially in hot weather, and replace fluids gradually afterward.

The effects of mild dehydration aren't limited to exercise — they also show up in everyday activities like work or study, through reduced concentration and alertness, headaches, mood changes, and skin that feels less fresh. This is one reason building a habit of drinking water regularly throughout the day — rather than waiting until you're thirsty to drink a lot at once — is more beneficial than treating hydration as only relevant during exercise.

Plain water isn't the only daily fluid source — about 20% of the average person's fluid needs are already met through food, especially high-water-content fruit and vegetables (watermelon, cucumber, tomato), soup, and milk and yogurt. These sources should be seen as a supplement, not a main substitute for plain water.

Besides urine color, other signs your body needs more fluid: dry mouth and lips, headache with no clear cause, unexplained fatigue or lethargy, noticeably less frequent urination, and dizziness when standing up too quickly. If several of these signs appear together, especially after physical activity or in hot weather, increase your fluid intake right away.

## Myth: "Drinking Water Makes You Fat"

This claim has no physiological basis — plain water has **zero calories**, so it can't directly contribute to weight gain in the form of fat. This misconception likely comes from two things:

1. **Temporary water retention** — the body can feel "bloated" or the scale can tick up slightly from water retention, especially with high salt intake or around a woman's menstrual cycle. This is temporary and not actual fat gain.
2. **Confusion with other drinks** — many people equate "drinking a lot" with sweet or caloric drinks like juice, sweet tea, or energy drinks, which do contribute significant calories — the plain water itself isn't the problem, it's the calories from what's mixed into it.

In fact, some research finds that drinking water before a meal can help reduce calorie intake during that meal, likely from partially filling stomach volume before solid food arrives — though the effect varies between individuals and isn't a magic weight-loss strategy on its own.

Fluid needs also aren't uniform depending on your environment: air-conditioned rooms have lower humidity, which increases fluid loss through evaporation even without feeling sweaty, so many office workers underestimate their fluid needs simply because they don't feel "sweaty" the way they would outdoors. A simple habit that helps: keep a water bottle at your desk as a visual reminder, and schedule specific drinking times instead of relying on thirst alone.

## Can You Drink Too Much Water?

Though rare in healthy people during everyday activity, consuming extremely large amounts of water in a short time (several liters within a few hours) can cause **hyponatremia** — blood sodium becoming too diluted. This risk is most relevant for marathon runners or ultra-endurance athletes, not the general population.

The idea that coffee and tea "cause dehydration" due to caffeine's diuretic effect is often overstated — at moderate intake (1-3 cups a day), the effect is relatively mild and the fluid still contributes positively to total daily intake. Alcohol is a different story: its diuretic effect is much stronger, one reason drinking a lot of alcohol without alternating with water can cause dehydration that contributes to next-day "hangover" symptoms.

Plain water is enough for most everyday hydration needs, but electrolyte drinks are more appropriate for high-intensity exercise longer than 60 minutes (especially in hot weather, since sweat carries out sodium and other minerals), diarrhea or vomiting (where oral rehydration salts are far more suitable), and prolonged heavy physical activity in very hot weather. For everyday activity and exercise under an hour, plain water remains the top choice.

## Practical Summary

- Fluid needs are individual — use urine color as a practical indicator, not a rigid "8 glasses a day" rule.
- Even mild dehydration (around 2% of body weight) can measurably reduce physical and cognitive performance.
- For exercise over an hour or in hot weather, consider an electrolyte drink, not just plain water.
- Plain water doesn't cause weight gain — zero calories. Temporary water retention or calories from other sweet drinks are usually the real cause behind this belief.
