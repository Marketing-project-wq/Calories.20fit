Of all the macronutrients, protein is the one people talk about most — "you need 2x your body weight," "it has to be right after your workout," "plant protein isn't complete enough." Some of that is true, some is exaggerated, and some is outdated compared to the latest evidence. This article summarizes how much protein you actually need daily, both for average people and active gym-goers, easy-to-find protein sources in Indonesia, and how much "protein timing" really matters — a topic that's often overblown.

## What Does Protein Actually Do in the Body?

Protein is made up of amino acids — 20 types in total, nine of which are called essential because the body can't produce them on its own and must get them from food. Its role goes far beyond just "building muscle":

- **Tissue repair and formation** — muscle, skin, hair, and internal organs are constantly being repaired and replaced, and protein is the raw material for that.
- **Enzyme and hormone production** — many digestive enzymes and hormones (including insulin) are made of protein.
- **Immune system** — antibodies that fight infection are also protein-based.
- **Fullness** — of the three macronutrients, protein has the highest satiety effect per calorie, which is why it's often recommended to help control appetite during a calorie deficit.

## How Much Protein Do You Actually Need Daily?

Indonesia's Ministry of Health sets the baseline protein requirement (AKG) at around **0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day** for adults who don't exercise much — consistent with WHO and RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) guidance in many countries, and considered enough to prevent deficiency in most healthy people.

But "enough to prevent deficiency" is different from "optimal for a specific goal." For people who exercise regularly, especially with weight training, the need is higher:

- **Light-moderate activity (walking, casual exercise)**: around 0.8–1.0 g/kg body weight.
- **Regular strength training, wanting to maintain muscle**: around 1.2–1.6 g/kg body weight, per the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand.
- **Intense strength training or in a calorie deficit (wanting to maintain muscle while losing fat)**: the ISSN recommends up to 1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight — the deeper the calorie deficit, the more important high protein becomes to prevent muscle loss.

> The "2 grams per kilogram" figure that circulates isn't entirely a myth — it sits at the upper end of the range research recommends for people doing intense strength training, not a mandatory number for everyone.

### A Sample Calculation

For someone weighing 65 kg who does regular strength training 3-4 times a week, their protein target is roughly 65 × 1.4 = **91 grams of protein per day**, which can be spread evenly across 3-4 meals.

## Easy-to-Find Protein Sources in Indonesia

Protein comes in two broad categories, and both can fully meet your needs as long as you get enough variety:

**Animal sources** (generally "complete proteins" — containing all essential amino acids in a single source):
- Eggs (~6–7 g protein each) — one of the most efficient and affordable protein sources.
- Skinless chicken breast (~23 g per 100 g).
- Fish like mackerel, catfish, tuna, and milkfish (~20–22 g per 100 g) — cheap, easy to find, and rich in omega-3.
- Tempeh and tofu are technically plant-based but are often grouped here because of their high protein content for plant foods.
- Milk and dairy products (yogurt, cheese).

**Plant sources:**
- Tempeh (~19 g per 100 g) and tofu (~8 g per 100 g) — fermented soybean products, rich in protein and probiotics from the fermentation process.
- Legumes: mung beans, red beans, peanuts.
- Edamame and lentils.

### On "Plant Protein Isn't Complete"

The claim that plant protein is "incomplete" and therefore inferior is often misunderstood. It's true that some single plant sources are lower in one or two essential amino acids, but this isn't a problem as long as your overall diet is varied — combinations like rice with legumes, or tempeh with vegetables and whole grains, naturally complement each other's amino acid profiles across the day. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics states that a well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can meet adult protein needs, including for athletes, without needing to combine protein sources within the same meal.

## How Important Is "Timing" Really?

The concept of the "anabolic window" — the belief that protein must be consumed within 30-60 minutes after exercise or its benefits are lost — was popular in the 2000s, but more recent evidence shows that window is much more flexible than most people believe.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating protein timing studies have found that **total daily protein intake** is a far more important factor for muscle growth than the specific timing of when it's consumed — as long as protein intake is spread reasonably evenly through the day, rather than concentrated in a single meal.

Better-supported takeaways:

- **Even distribution beats stacking.** Splitting protein intake across 3-4 meals (around 20-40 grams per meal) appears more effective for muscle protein synthesis than eating most of your daily protein in one large meal.
- **The real window is much wider than 30 minutes.** As long as protein is consumed within a few hours before or after a training session (not immediately after), its benefits for recovery and muscle growth still apply.
- **For non-competitive athletes**, focusing on sufficient, consistent total daily intake matters far more than chasing minute-precise timing.

> If you have to choose between "protein exactly 30 minutes after the gym" versus "enough protein spread evenly through the day," pick the second — it has a much bigger impact on long-term results.

## Can You Have Too Much Protein?

For adults with normal kidney function, research hasn't found strong evidence that high protein intake (within the range recommended by sports nutrition research, up to around 2-2.2 g/kg) is harmful to the kidneys. Concerns about "protein damaging your kidneys" generally come from studies on people who already had existing kidney impairment, not healthy populations — in that group, protein restriction is indeed relevant and should be discussed with a doctor or dietitian.

A more realistic concern is calorie balance: protein still provides about 4 kcal per gram, so intake far exceeding your needs can still contribute to a calorie surplus if not balanced against total daily intake.

## Practical Summary

- Baseline need: around 0.8 g/kg body weight for inactive people; rising to 1.2–2.2 g/kg for those doing regular strength training, depending on intensity and whether they're in a calorie deficit.
- Vary your animal and plant sources — tempeh, tofu, eggs, fish, and chicken are all affordable options in Indonesia.
- Spread protein intake across several meals through the day, rather than chasing a narrow "window" after exercise.
- Focus on overall daily consistency — that's what matters most for long-term results, both for lasting fullness and preserving muscle mass.
