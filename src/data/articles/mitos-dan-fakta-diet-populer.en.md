On social media, a "new diet" seems to show up nearly every month claiming to be the miracle answer to weight loss: 16-hour fasting, cutting out carbs entirely, or drinking green juice to "cleanse toxins". These claims sound convincing, especially wrapped in dramatic testimonials. This article reviews some of the most popular diet approaches by referencing scientific evidence and systematic reviews, helping you understand what's actually supported by research rather than crowning any single approach as "correct".

## Intermittent Fasting: Not Metabolic Magic

**IF** governs when you eat, not what you eat — common variations include 16:8, 5:2, and alternate-day fasting. A review in NEJM summarizes the biological mechanisms (a shift toward ketones, autophagy), but evidence in humans for benefits beyond weight loss is still limited.

**Myth:** IF has a "magic" metabolic effect that burns fat more efficiently. **Fact:** When calories and macronutrients are matched, IF and continuous calorie restriction show similar weight-loss results. IF's edge lies in **behavior** — a narrow eating window causes many people to naturally eat less without counting every gram.

IF isn't for everyone — it's best avoided or discussed with a doctor first for: those with a history of eating disorders, people with diabetes on insulin, pregnant/breastfeeding women, and children/teens who are still growing.

## Keto Diet: Who It Can Work For

Keto (carbs <50g/day) triggers **ketosis** — the liver breaks down fat into ketones as an alternative fuel. It was originally developed for **refractory epilepsy**, and is still used under medical supervision for that purpose. Outside a medical context, some people find keto helps control appetite.

What to understand before trying it: the rapid early weight loss is mostly from water lost as muscle glycogen depletes, not fat — not evidence of extraordinary fat burning; long-term adherence is a major challenge, especially in Indonesia's rice-centered food culture; lipid profiles can shift differently between individuals, with some people seeing LDL cholesterol rise from high saturated fat; and its long-term cardiovascular effects are still being studied.

**Myth:** Keto is superior for fat burning because the body is "forced" to use fat. **Fact:** When calories and protein are matched, ISSN research finds no meaningful difference in fat loss results — the deciding factor remains total energy balance.

## Low-Carb vs. Low-Fat: Similar Results

The low-carb versus low-fat debate has gone on for decades. Large-scale meta-analyses and systematic reviews (The Lancet, Cochrane) pooling many randomized clinical trials find that low-carb and low-fat diets produce **generally similar** weight loss over the medium-to-long term, as long as calories are controlled at a comparable level. What actually determines the outcome is **adherence** — shaped by taste/cultural food preferences, each person's satiety response, and social practicality.

> There's no single universal "best diet". The best diet is whichever one is flexible and enjoyable enough to keep following.

## Detox and Cleanses: Weak Evidence

**Myth:** Detox/cleanse programs (special juices, slimming teas, liquid fasts) flush toxins from the body and give your metabolism a "reset". **Fact:** The human body already has a sophisticated detoxification system that works nonstop — the liver filters and neutralizes metabolic byproducts, the kidneys filter blood and remove waste through urine — without needing any "detox program" at all. There's no strong scientific evidence that detox juices or "cleansing" products speed up this process. The weight loss that sometimes occurs during a short-term detox comes from drastic calorie restriction and fluid loss, not "toxin removal". The risks are real: fatigue and electrolyte disturbances from extreme calorie restriction, muscle loss from liquid fasting without adequate protein, poorly regulated commercial products, and results that are temporary — once normal eating resumes, the weight lost (mostly water) comes right back.

## "Eating Late Makes You Fat": A Myth That Won't Die

One of the most persistent myths: eating after a certain hour automatically makes you gain weight, as if the body somehow "stores" the same calories as fat more easily at night. In fact, the body has no biological clock that suddenly makes the same calories more fattening just because they were eaten at night versus during the day. **Total calories consumed across the whole day**, compared to energy burned, remains the deciding factor — regardless of what time those calories went in. What keeps this myth alive is a real behavior pattern: evening eating is often associated with mindless snacking in front of a screen, much larger portions after holding off hunger all day, or easier access to high-calorie foods like fried snacks as night approaches. There's also another consideration: eating a large portion too close to bedtime can disrupt sleep quality — a valid reason to manage your dinner timing, but about sleep comfort, not about calories "piling up faster".

## "Carbs Are the Enemy": Oversimplifying Something Complex

**Myth:** Carbohydrates in general cause weight gain, so anyone who wants to be healthy should minimize them. **Fact:** What matters far more for health and weight is the **type** and **amount** of carbohydrate, not its mere presence. Refined carbs (added sugar, white flour, sugary drinks, ultra-processed food) digest quickly, are low in fiber, and are easy to overeat because they're less filling per calorie. Whole carbs (whole grains, tubers, legumes, fruit, vegetables) come with fiber, vitamins, and minerals, digest more slowly, and are more filling. For most healthy people, there's no strong reason to avoid rice, potatoes, or fruit entirely — what matters more is portion size and balancing them with protein and fiber.

## How to Evaluate a Viral Diet

Some **red flags**: promises of extreme results in a very short time, banning entire food groups without a clear medical reason, relying on testimonials and before-after photos instead of scientific evidence, ultimately selling an exclusive product, and not being sustainable in everyday life.

> A credible diet usually doesn't need drama — it's simple, flexible, and physiologically makes sense.

## Bottom Line

Almost every diet that works does so through the same mechanism: a calorie deficit that can be sustained consistently over the long term. IF, keto, low-carb, or low-fat are just different tools toward the same goal — pick whichever is most flexible to live with, not whichever is most viral or extreme.
