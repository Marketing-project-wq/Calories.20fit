If you've ever asked "how many calories do I actually need a day?", the answer isn't as simple as the generic number floating around social media. Everyone's daily calorie needs differ, depending on weight, height, age, sex, and how active your daily life is. Fortunately, there are scientifically validated formulas for estimating it.

## BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate)

**BMR** is the minimum number of calories your body needs for vital functions at complete rest — not sleep, but being awake with zero physical activity: your heart beating, lungs breathing, brain working, body temperature staying stable, cells renewing. Even lying still all day, your body still burns roughly this many calories. BMR is usually the largest share of total daily calories. Factors that affect it: muscle mass (muscle tissue is more metabolically active, so more muscle means higher BMR), age (BMR declines with age due to natural muscle loss), sex (men are generally higher due to greater muscle proportion), and body size (a bigger body needs more energy).

### The Mifflin-St Jeor Formula

- Men: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
- Women: BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

This formula (Mifflin MD, St Jeor ST, 1990, American Journal of Clinical Nutrition) has been shown to be more accurate than the older Harris-Benedict formula (1919), and is the reference used by many modern calorie calculators, including 20FIT.

### Why Mifflin-St Jeor Is More Accurate

Before Mifflin-St Jeor became popular, the more commonly used formula was Harris-Benedict, developed in 1919 and revised in 1984 from a much smaller sample and early-20th-century body composition. Studies comparing predicted results against direct measurement (indirect calorimetry) have found that Mifflin-St Jeor produces estimates closer to actual values, including in people carrying excess weight.

## From BMR to TDEE

**TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure)** is the total number of calories your body burns in 24 hours, made up of four components: **BMR** (usually the largest component), **TEF** (Thermic Effect of Food — the energy used to digest food; protein has the highest thermic effect), **NEAT** (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis — energy from daily activity outside scheduled exercise, like walking or tidying up), and **structured exercise**. A practical way to estimate TDEE: multiply BMR by an **activity multiplier**:

- Sedentary (rarely active): ×1.2
- Light (exercise 1-3x/week): ×1.375
- Moderate (3-5x/week): ×1.55
- Active (6-7x/week): ×1.725
- Very active (athlete): ×1.9

### Example: Woman, 30 Years Old, 60 kg, 160 cm, Moderate Activity

BMR = (10×60) + (6.25×160) − (5×30) − 161 = 600 + 1,000 − 150 − 161 = 1,289 kcal. TDEE = 1,289 × 1.55 ≈ **2,000 kcal/day** to maintain a stable weight of 60 kg. For men, the formula uses "+5" instead of "−161" in the last step — a small difference in the formula, but the result can differ quite a bit because men typically carry more muscle mass at the same body weight.

## Calorie Deficit and Surplus

A **deficit** happens when calories consumed are less than TDEE — the body draws extra energy from fat stores (and potentially muscle), resulting in weight loss. A **surplus** is the opposite — more calories are consumed than TDEE, and the excess is stored by the body, either as muscle (if paired with strength training and enough protein) or fat (if not). Neither is inherently good or bad — it depends on the goal: losing weight, maintaining it, or building muscle.

According to the Mayo Clinic, a deficit of **~500 kcal/day** is a common, safe recommendation that's realistic to sustain long term, equal to roughly 0.5 kg/week of loss (1 kg of body fat ≈ 7,700 kcal of energy; 500×7 = 3,500 kcal deficit/week ÷ 7,700 ≈ 0.45 kg). A range of 500-1,000 kcal/day is generally considered safe for most adults, but 500 kcal is more moderate, easier to sustain in daily life, and carries less risk than cutting 1,000 kcal all at once.

> A healthy deficit isn't about who loses weight fastest — it's about how long that deficit can be sustained consistently.

## The Risk of Too Extreme a Deficit

Cutting calories too drastically backfires:

- **Metabolic adaptation (adaptive thermogenesis)** — a body under a deep, prolonged deficit responds by lowering energy expenditure beyond what weight loss alone would explain. NEAT drops because a person unconsciously moves less, so weight loss slows or stalls even while eating very little.
- **Muscle loss** — without enough protein and strength training, the body tends to break down muscle tissue as an extra energy source, not just fat — lowering BMR long-term because muscle burns more calories than fat.
- **Declining performance** — excessive fatigue, poor concentration, mood disturbances. The ACSM warns against diets below 800 kcal/day without medical supervision.
- **The yo-yo effect** — extreme eating patterns are hard to sustain long-term; once they stop (from physical/mental exhaustion), hunger increases while metabolism is still adapting, making it easy for weight to come back, sometimes exceeding the starting weight.

## The Formula's Numbers Are Just a Starting Estimate

The Mifflin-St Jeor formula and activity multipliers are statistical generalizations from population data, not a direct measurement of any one specific body. Factors like genetics, body composition, hormonal status, sleep quality, stress levels, and even past dieting history can make actual BMR and TDEE differ somewhat from the formula's result. Treat this number as a starting estimate, not a fixed rule: apply it consistently for 2-4 weeks, track your weight regularly at the same time each day (look at the weekly average, not the day-to-day ups and downs), compare the actual result to the prediction, then adjust up or down by roughly 100-200 kcal if it's off by a lot after being applied consistently.

> The right calorie target isn't the lowest one — it's the one you can actually sustain consistently over time.

Bottom line: calculate BMR, multiply by the activity factor for TDEE, adjust based on your goal (a ~500 kcal deficit for weight loss, a moderate surplus for building muscle), then track and adjust based on how your body responds — not a fixed rule set in stone.
